# Dive B: cognitive science and math models of individual decision-makers
Written 2026-09-11. Question: which compact per-person models of *how* someone thinks (search depth, noise, lapses, time use) could give our clone engine the "feel" of one specific player, fittable from 50 to 5,000 games on a laptop CPU.
"Verified Y" means the PDF is in `lit/pdf/`, its page-1 title was checked, and the quoted number was found in the extracted text. Text dumps were made with pymupdf; nothing heavy was run.

## Top 5 ideas from cognitive science / math for user-games → clone-engine (ranked)

**1. Couple think-time to difficulty and error (the joint time–quality check, then a fix if needed).**
Sunde 2026 and Russek 2025 show that within one player, long thinks happen in positions where thinking pays off and still produce worse moves. Anderson 2016 shows 3-minute blunder rates are flat above ~10 s and climb steeply below. First measure on held-out games whether our clone reproduces three things: (a) the correlation between think-time and value of computation (VOC), (b) the drop in move quality as think-time rises, and (c) the shape of blunder rate vs seconds left. If the time sample and the move sample are drawn independently, condition the move on the sampled time (or the reverse).
Benefit: high for feel. It targets the "it thinks long and then plays well" giveaway. Data: none new; population-level held-out data plus Stockfish at 2 depths. CPU: fine. Risk: low, because it starts as a measurement.

**2. Partial pooling for every per-player scalar.**
This covers temperature, the two timing scalars, and any error knob. Estimate each as a precision-weighted blend: player value × n/(n+k) + population value × k/(n+k), with k set from the between-player variance. HDDM shows the gains are largest at few trials. Katahira shows reliability must be judged across time periods. van Opheusden shows even a good model's parameters correlate only 0.5–0.58 across sessions.
Benefit: prevents 50-game users from getting noisy, overfitted clones. It replaces hand-tuned scales. Data: the existing player pool. CPU: trivial (closed form). Risk: low.

**3. A per-player "time policy" built from value of computation, replacing ad-hoc timing scalars.**
Use two numbers: a base pace, and a slope of log think-time on value of computation, where VOC = the eval gap between deep and shallow Stockfish. Add Sigman's budget-use fraction as a check. These are readable, fittable from a few hundred moves, and grounded in 12.5 M games.
Benefit: medium-high for time feel. It also gives an honest "does this player know when to think" dial. Data: Stockfish at depth 1 and ~12 per position for the user's games, about 2k–200k evals, feasible on CPU overnight at the high end. Risk: the Elo effect is "modest", so individual slopes may be noisy. Pool them (idea 2).

**4. A Regan-style mistake-shape layer (sensitivity s, consistency c, plus a clock term) on top of the neural policy, middlegame only.**
Fit per player how likely they are to play a move losing δ centipawns, given clock and difficulty. Use it only as a small logit correction (tilt the policy toward or away from lossy moves), not as a move generator; Maia shows noisy engines are poor movers.
Benefit: controls blunder frequency and size, which is what the owner calls "mistake pattern". Data: engine evals of the user's moves, a few hundred moves for 2 parameters. CPU: yes. Risk: medium-high. Anderson and Sigman say blunders are mostly difficulty × clock and weakly per-person, so the fitted s and c may mostly re-encode Elo, which we already condition on. Test by first checking whether s and c are stable across a player's time splits.

**5. A van Opheusden-style search wrapper with per-player depth and lapse parameters.**
Wrap the neural policy and value in a shallow best-first search with 3–4 per-player parameters: stopping probability (depth), pruning threshold, lapse (random feature or branch drop), and noise. Fit them with inverse binomial sampling or ABC (Kangasrääsiö) against the user's moves and think-times. In their data it gives both moves (40.8 %) and RT (ρ = 0.35) from one mechanism, and it passes a Turing test (55.4 %). UCLouvain (NOTES.md #6) also found search improves style.
Benefit: potentially the only route to "midgame personality" and time use from a single mechanism. Data: a few hundred to a few thousand positions. CPU: slow, because likelihood-free fitting needs many simulations per position (hours per user). Risk: high. Across-session reliability is only about 0.5, lapse rates are not reliably identifiable, and chess is far bigger than four-in-a-row.

## Three most decision-changing facts
1. **Position difficulty, not the person, drives mistakes.** Anderson 2016: difficulty features are "significantly more powerful than features based on skill or time", and 3-minute blunder rates are flat above ~10 s left. Sigman 2010: blunder counts are "insufficient to classify a player". So a per-player blunder knob is low-yield, and getting the population clock × difficulty curve right matters more.
2. **About half of a person's "thinking style" is not stable over time.** van Opheusden: the most reliable per-person parameters correlate 0.93 on refits but only 0.53–0.58 across sessions of the same person, and the lapse rate is not reliable at all. Our ≤0.4 pp middlegame personalization ceiling is partly the person drifting, not a model defect. Shrinkage and time-split validation are mandatory.
3. **Within the same player, slower moves are worse moves** (Sunde, PNAS 2026, with player-game fixed effects; strongest in Blitz and Rapid). Players also spend time where computation pays (Russek). A clone that samples think-time independently of move quality will feel wrong. The coupling is the thing to check first.
